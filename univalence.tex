\section{Introduction}

This document is about some gory details of 
Voevodsky's univalence axiom for homotopy type theory (HoTT).
We follow 
\citep[hereinafter referred to as the HoTT book]{hott-book}
\defcitealias{hott-book}{the HoTT book}
and \citet{rijke}.

\section{Identity Types}

For $x, y : A$ we have the identity type $(x =_A y)$ also written
$\text{Id}_A(x, y)$.  The latter means we can think of $\text{Id}_A$
as a function $A \to A \to \universe$.

Identity on a type $A$ is an equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric,
and transitive), that is, the types
\begin{subequations}
\begin{gather}
   (x =_A x)
   \label{eq:path-reflexivity}
   \\
   (x =_A y) \to (y =_A x)
   \label{eq:path-symmetry}
   \\
   (x =_A y) \to (y =_A z) \to (x =_A z)
   \label{eq:path-transitivity}
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}
are all inhabited
\citepalias[definition of $\refl$ and Lemmas~2.1.1 and~2.1.2]{hott-book}.
An inhabitant of \eqref{eq:path-reflexivity} is denoted $\refl_x$
(there may be other inhabitants).
An inhabitant of \eqref{eq:path-symmetry} is denoted $p \mapsto p^{-1}$.
An inhabitant of \eqref{eq:path-transitivity}
is denoted $(p, q) \mapsto p \cdot q$.

If we think of $A$ as a category, terms of $A$ as objects of the category,
and terms of $(x =_A y)$ as morphisms from $x$ to $y$, then these morphisms
satisfy the category axioms with $\refl_x$ being the identity morphism for
object $x$ and each morphism being iso \citepalias[Lemma~2.1.4]{hott-book}.
Thus this category is a groupoid.  Actually, this category is
an $\infty$-groupoid, but this lemma does not assert that.

\subsection{Path Lifting}

For any function $f : A \to B$ there exists
$$
   \text{ap}_f : (x =_A y) \to \bigl(f(x) =_B f(y)\bigr)
$$
which lifts a path $p$ in $A$ to a path $\text{ap}_f(p)$ in $B$
such that
$$
   \text{ap}_f(\refl_x) = \refl_{f(x)}
$$
\citepalias[Lemma~2.2.1]{hott-book}.
When no confusion will result, the HoTT book and others use the
abuse of notation $f(p)$ rather $\text{ap}_f(p)$.
This, strictly speaking, makes no sense because $p$ is not a point in the
domain of $f$ nor even made out of points of the domain of $f$.
But if we consider a path to be a subset of the type
(as it would be classically)
and let $f(p)$ denote the image of the subset $p$,
which is a widely used mathematical notation, then this makes sense.

If we continue thinking of $A$ and $B$ as categories with their paths as
morphisms, then $f$ is the part of a functor between these categories
that maps objects to objects and $\text{ap}_f$ is the part of the functor
that maps morphisms to morphisms.  In order for this to hold we need
$$
   f(p \cdot q) = f(p) \cdot f(q)
$$
\citepalias[Lemma~2.2.2(i); this lemma also asserts other properties
of path lifting]{hott-book}.

There is also path lifting for dependent functions
$$
   f : \prod_{x : A} B(x)
$$
for which there exists for every path $p : (x =_A y)$ a function
$$
   p_{\textstyle *} : B(x) \to B(y)
$$
\citepalias[Lemma~2.3.1]{hott-book}.
This function is so important that the HoTT book gives it another
notation
$$
   \text{transport}^B(p, \fatdot) \equiv p_{\textstyle *}(\fatdot)
$$
There are many other properties related to path lifting in
Section~2.3 of \citet{hott-book}, but we will skip them for now.

\section{Homotopies}

Given two dependent functions of the same type
$$
   f, g : \prod_{x : A} B(x)
$$
a \emph{homotopy} between them is a dependent function of type
$$
   (f \sim g) :\equiv \prod_{x : A} \bigl(f(x) =_{B(x)} g(x)\bigr)
$$
(\citetalias[Definition~2.4.1]{hott-book};
\citealp[Definition~9.1.2]{rijke}).

A homotopy is (as the language above says) a dependent function
with domain $A$.  Hence, if $H : (f \sim g)$,
then $H(x) : \bigl(f(x) =_{B(x)} g(x)\bigr)$,
that is, $H(x)$ is a path from $f(x)$ to $g(x)$.

Homotopy is an equivalence relation, that is, if $f$, $g$, and $h$
are dependent functions of the same type
\begin{subequations}
\begin{gather}
   (f \sim f)
   \label{eq:homotopy-reflexivity}
   \\
   (f \sim g) \to (g \sim f)
   \label{eq:homotopy-symmetry}
   \\
   (f \sim g) \to (g \sim h) \to (f \sim h)
   \label{eq:homotopy-transitivity}
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}
\citepalias[Lemma~2.4.2]{hott-book}.

Since \citet{hott-book} leaves the proof of this lemma to the reader,
we do it.  For \eqref{eq:homotopy-reflexivity}, $x \mapsto \refl_{f(x)}$
does the job.  For \eqref{eq:homotopy-symmetry}, given $H : (f \sim g)$
we find $x \mapsto H(x)^{-1}$ does the job.
For \eqref{eq:homotopy-transitivity}, given $H : (f \sim g)$ and
$J : (g \to h)$ that $x \mapsto H(x) \cdot J(x)$ is in $(f \sim h)$.

For $f, g : A \to B$ and $H : (f \sim g)$ and $p : x =_A y$ we have
$$
   H(x) \cdot g(p) = f(p) \cdot H(y)
$$
where $f(p)$ and $g(p)$ are, more pedantically,
$\text{ap}_f(p)$ and $\text{ap}_g(p)$
\citepalias[Lemma~2.4.2]{hott-book}.

\section{Homotopy Equivalence}

There are multiple different homotopy equivalent ways to define homotopy
equivalence.  The point of the univalence axiom is that that they are
all equally good.  Section~2.4 of \citetalias{hott-book} and Definition~9.2.1
of \citet{rijke} define homotopy equivalence to be the property of a map
being \emph{bi-invertible}, that is, for $f : A \to B$
$$
   \biinv(f) :\equiv
   \left( \sum_{g : B \to A} (f \circ g \sim \idfun_B) \right)
   \times
   \left( \sum_{h : B \to A} (h \circ f \sim \idfun_A) \right)
$$
\citepalias[equation (2.4.10)]{hott-book}
that is, up to homotopy, $f$ has a left inverse $h$ and a right inverse $g$.
Later on (Section~4.5) \citetalias{hott-book} redefines homotopy equivalence
to be another (homotopy equivalent) property.  It does not matter which
homotopy equivalent property is used to define homotopy equivalence.
Hence we take
$$
   \isequiv(f) := \biinv(f)
$$

A related definition is \emph{quasi-inverse:} for $f : A \to B$
$$
   \qinv(f) :\equiv \sum_{g : B \to A}
   (f \circ g \sim \idfun_B) \times (g \circ f \sim \idfun_A)
$$
\citepalias[equation~(2.4.5)]{hott-book}.
This, it turns out, is not a mere property
\citepalias[Theorem~4.1.3]{hott-book} and also not satisfactory for
defining the univalence axiom
\citepalias[Exercise~4.6]{hott-book}.
But it is useful for understanding homotopy equivalence.

For $f : A \to B$
\begin{subequations}
\begin{gather}
   \qinv(f) \to \isequiv(f)
   \label{eq:qinv-to-isequiv}
   \\
   \isequiv(f) \to \qinv(f)
   \label{eq:isequiv-to-qinv}
   \\
   \text{$\isequiv(f)$ is a mere proposition}
   \label{eq:isequiv-proposition}
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}
The first two of these say $\qinv(f)$ and $\isequiv(f)$ are logically
equivalent in the sense of propositions-as-types and proof-relevant logic
(since one is a mere proposition and the other not).

Proofs of the first two are found in Section~2.4 of \citetalias{hott-book}.
First we note that an inhabitant of $\qinv(f)$, where $f : A \to B$,
is $(g, (G, H))$, where $g : B \to A$ and $G$ and $H$ are homotopies
\begin{align*}
   G & : (f \circ g \sim \idfun_B)
   \\
   H & : (g \circ f \sim \idfun_A)
\end{align*}
And an inhabitant of $\biinv(f)$, where $f : A \to B$,
is $((g, G), (h, H))$, where $g, h : B \to A$ and $G$ and $H$ are homotopies
\begin{align*}
   G & : (f \circ g \sim \idfun_B)
   \\
   H & : (h \circ f \sim \idfun_A)
\end{align*}
We can also think of these as tuples: $(g, G, H)$ and $(g, G, h, H)$.

An inhabitant of \eqref{eq:qinv-to-isequiv}
is $(g, G, H) \mapsto (g, G, g, H)$ if $\qinv(f)$ is inhabited and
$0 \to \isequiv(f)$ otherwise.
An inhabitant of \eqref{eq:isequiv-to-qinv} is more complicated.
Given $(g, G, h, H) : \isequiv(f)$ \citetalias{hott-book} defines
$$
   J(y) :\equiv H(g(y))^{-1} \cdot h(G(y))
$$
and declares $J$ is a homotopy.  What type is it?
\begin{gather*}
   G(y) : ((f \circ g)(y) = y)
   \\
   h(G(y)) : ((h \circ f \circ g)(y) = h(y))
   \\
   H(x) : (h \circ f)(x) = x)
   \\
   H(g(y)) : ((h \circ f \circ g)(y) = g(y))
   \\
   H(g(y))^{-1} : (g(y) = (h \circ f \circ g)(y))
   \\
   J(y) \equiv H(g(y))^{-1} \cdot h(G(y)) : (g(y) = h(y))
\end{gather*}
Thus $J : (g \sim h)$.

Now define
$$
   K(x) :\equiv J(f(x)) \cdot H(x)
$$
\citetalias{hott-book} then claims $(g, G, K) : \qinv(f)$.  Check that.
That $G$ proves $g$ is a right inverse of $f$ is true by assumption.
So we have to show that $K$ proves $g$ is a left inverse of $f$.
\begin{gather*}
   J(f(x)) : ((g \circ f)(x) = (h \circ f)(x))
   \\
   K(x) \equiv J(f(x)) \cdot H(x) : ((g \circ f)(x) = x)
\end{gather*}
So indeed
$$
   K : (g \circ f \sim \idfun_A)
$$
(If it type checks, then it is a valid proof!)
We still have to do the case where $\biinv(f)$ is not inhabited,
but it is again trivial.
